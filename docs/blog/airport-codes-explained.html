Why is Orlando MCO? The stories behind airport codes

October 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Your bag tag says MCO, but you're flying to Orlando. Your friend lands at ORD, which is somehow Chicago. Airport codes look random until you learn where they came from. Then most of them turn into tiny history lessons.

Who hands out the codes

The three-letter codes on boarding passes and bag tags are assigned by IATA, the International Air Transport Association, the trade group for the world's airlines. Each code points to one place, so a bag tagged SFO never gets mixed up with one headed somewhere else. Airline reservation systems, baggage belts and booking sites all run on these codes.

There's a second system you'll rarely see as a passenger. Pilots and air traffic controllers use four-letter ICAO codes. In the lower 48 US states, most big airports simply add a K to the front of the familiar code, so LAX becomes KLAX. Other countries follow their own regional patterns: London Heathrow is EGLL.

Old names that stuck

Many codes come from what an airport used to be called. The name changed, but the code stayed.

  • ORD, Chicago O'Hare: the field was once Orchard Field, and the code never caught up with the new name.
  • MCO, Orlando: the airport grew out of the former McCoy Air Force Base.
  • MSY, New Orleans: from Moisant Stock Yards, the land the airport was built on.
  • BNA, Nashville: from Berry Field Nashville, its old name.
  • MCI, Kansas City: short for Mid-Continent International.

It works the same way abroad. Beijing is PEK, from Peking, the old English spelling of the city's name. Mumbai is BOM, from Bombay.

Renaming an airport rarely changes its code, because so many systems depend on it. Las Vegas switched from McCarran to Harry Reid International in 2021 and stayed LAS. The famous exception is New York's JFK. It was Idlewild, code IDL, until it was renamed for President John F. Kennedy in December 1963, and the code changed with it.

The town next door

Big airports are often built outside the city they serve, and some codes name the town they're actually in. Rome's main airport is FCO, for Fiumicino. São Paulo's is GRU, for Guarulhos. Buenos Aires has EZE, for Ezeiza, and Rio de Janeiro's GIG comes from Galeão.

Cincinnati's airport, CVG, takes it a step further: it isn't even in Ohio. It sits across the river in Kentucky, near Covington. And Iceland's main international airport, KEF, is in Keflavík, about 50 km from Reykjavík.

Filler letters and off-limits letters

Early airport codes had just two letters. When the industry moved to three, some airports simply added one. That's the usual explanation for LAX: Los Angeles was LA, and the X is filler.

Other odd codes come from letters that were already spoken for. In the US, commercial airport codes generally avoid K and W, the letters radio and TV stations use for their call signs, and codes starting with N are traditionally kept for Navy airfields. So Newark became EWR and Norfolk became ORF, built from the middle of the city's name.

Why Canadian codes start with Y

Toronto is YYZ, Vancouver is YVR and Montreal is YUL. Nearly every major Canadian airport starts with Y. The most common explanation goes back to old weather and radio station codes: Canada put a Y in front of existing two-letter station codes, which is why the other two letters often seem unrelated to the city. Accounts differ on the details, but the pattern is so strong that a Y code is a safe bet for Canada.

YYZ even became a song. The Canadian band Rush named an instrumental after Toronto's code, and its opening rhythm spells out Y-Y-Z in Morse code.

Codes that just make sense

Plenty of codes are exactly what you'd guess: ATL, DEN, BOS, SEA, MIA. When a city has more than one airport, each one needs its own code. Washington has IAD for Dulles and DCA for Reagan National, which is usually said to stand for District of Columbia Airport. Houston's IAH puts an I for Intercontinental up front.

City codes: one code, several airports

Some cities also have a metropolitan code that covers all of their airports at once. NYC covers JFK, LaGuardia (LGA) and Newark (EWR). LON covers Heathrow, Gatwick and London's other airports. Some flight search sites accept these city codes, which is an easy way to compare every airport in an area. That's worth doing, because a different airport in the same region can be much cheaper.

How to remember the odd ones

  • Look for an old name. If a code makes no sense, the airport probably had a different name or used to be a military base.
  • Look for the suburb. Codes like FCO and GRU name the town the airport sits in, not the big city.
  • Spot the patterns. A Y up front means Canada. A K in front of a familiar code means you're reading a four-letter US code.
  • Say it out loud. MSY sounds nothing like New Orleans, but "Moisant Stock Yards" is memorable once you've heard it.
