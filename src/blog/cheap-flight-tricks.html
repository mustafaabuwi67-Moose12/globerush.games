Cheap-flight advice is full of myths, like the idea that one magic hour of the week unlocks secret fares. The tricks that really work are less mysterious. They come from how airlines price seats and how fees add up. Here are the patterns behind every case in Fare Detective.

A quick note on numbers: the prices below are examples of common patterns, not quotes. Real fares change by route, date and demand.

1. Don't wait until the last minute

Airlines sell the seats on a flight at several price levels. As the cheaper ones sell out, the seats that are left cost more, so fares tend to climb in the last two to three weeks before departure. For trips within the US, booking a few weeks to a couple of months ahead is often the sweet spot. Last-minute deals do happen, but they're not something to plan a vacation around.

2. Fly midweek

Friday and Sunday are busy days for vacationers and business travelers alike, so they tend to cost more. Leaving on a Tuesday or Wednesday is often noticeably cheaper for the same length of trip. If your dates are flexible, search with a calendar or flexible-date view and let the prices choose the days for you.

3. Check nearby airports

Many cities are served by more than one airport, and the smaller one can be much cheaper because budget airlines like to fly there. Fort Lauderdale (FLL), about 30 miles from Miami, is a classic alternative to Miami International. Long Beach (LGB) can beat Los Angeles (LAX). Add up the cost of the extra drive or train ride before you book, but you'll often still come out ahead.

4. Compare the total, not the fare

A $49 fare on a budget airline can turn into a more expensive trip than a $128 fare on a full-service airline. In one Fare Detective case, adding a carry-on bag and a seat next to a friend takes the budget ticket to $139, while the full-service fare already includes both. Bag and seat fees vary by airline and route, so price out the trip you'll actually take, with the bags you'll really bring, before you compare.

Packing light is the simplest way around bag fees. On many budget fares, only a small personal item that fits under the seat is free. Carry-On Fitter turns the packing part into a daily puzzle: fit every item into the suitcase with no empty squares.

The same goes for basic economy fares on the big airlines. Depending on the airline and route, they can limit seat choice, changes or even carry-on bags. Check what's included before you click the cheapest number.

5. Accept a connection

Nonstop flights are faster, and airlines know many people will pay extra for that. A one-stop itinerary on the same route is often cheaper, sometimes by hundreds of dollars on long trips such as flights across the Atlantic. If a few extra hours don't bother you, it's one of the most reliable ways to save. Just leave yourself enough time to make the connection, especially if you have to go through customs on the way.

6. Try two one-way tickets

On US domestic routes, a round-trip ticket is usually priced like two one-ways stuck together. That means you can mix airlines: fly out on whichever is cheapest that day and come back on another. Long international trips can work differently, and two one-ways sometimes cost more than a round trip there, so compare both before you buy.

7. Travel in shoulder season

Peak summer is usually the most expensive time to fly to Europe. Shoulder season, roughly spring and early fall, often has much lower fares, smaller crowds and still-pleasant weather. A week in Paris in late September can cost far less than the same week in mid-July.

8. Fly into a hub, then drive

Small airports have few airlines and little competition, so fares stay high. Heading to the slopes near Aspen? Flying into Denver, a major hub served by many airlines, and taking a shuttle for the last few hours can cost about half as much, even after you pay for the ride. The same idea works for plenty of small mountain, beach and island airports.

Hubs are also why so many trips involve a connection at all. Airlines route passengers through a handful of big airports, and knowing which hubs serve your area helps you spot smarter routings.

A few habits that help

  • Set a fare alert for routes you care about, so you hear about price drops without checking every day.
  • Know the 24-hour rule. For flights to, from or within the US, airlines must let you either hold a fare or cancel without penalty for 24 hours after booking, as long as you book directly with the airline at least seven days before departure. Most airlines choose free cancellation, and some go further, so check the airline's own policy.
  • Read the fee list. Checked bags, seat assignments and, on some budget airlines, even checking in at the airport can cost extra.
  • Be flexible on one thing. If you can bend on the day, the airport or the number of stops, you'll usually find a better price than if every detail is fixed.
