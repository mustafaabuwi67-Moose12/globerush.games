Some flags are so similar that even geography fans mix them up. That's not always an accident: neighboring countries often share history, colors and symbols, and there are only so many simple designs to go around. Here's how to tell the trickiest pairs apart.

Chad and Romania

This is the hardest pair in the world. Both are vertical stripes of blue, yellow and red, in the same order. The only real difference is the shade: Chad's blue is a little darker. Unless you see them side by side, there's no reliable way to tell them apart at a glance, so use context, like the language on the page or what's around the flag.

Indonesia and Monaco

Both flags are a red stripe over a white one. The difference is the shape. Indonesia's flag has 2:3 proportions, while Monaco's is 4:5, so Monaco's looks squarer. The reds differ slightly too. And if the white is on top, it's neither: that's Poland.

Ireland, Côte d'Ivoire and Italy

Three vertical stripes with white in the middle. Ireland is green, white and orange, with green next to the pole. Côte d'Ivoire flips it: orange next to the pole, then white, then green. Italy swaps the orange for red: green, white and red. A memory trick for Ireland: green comes first, like its nickname, the Emerald Isle.

Australia and New Zealand

Both are dark blue, with the British Union Jack in the top corner and the Southern Cross constellation on the right. Look at the stars.

  • Australia: six white stars. A large seven-pointed star, the Commonwealth Star, sits under the Union Jack, and the Southern Cross has five white stars.
  • New Zealand: four red stars with white borders, and no star under the Union Jack.

White stars, and more of them: Australia. Red stars: New Zealand.

The Netherlands and Luxembourg

Both are horizontal stripes of red, white and blue. Luxembourg's blue is a lighter sky blue, and its flag is a bit longer. The Netherlands uses a darker blue. Russia uses the same three colors in a different order: white, blue and red from top to bottom.

The Nordic crosses

Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland and Iceland all use a cross that's shifted toward the pole, called the Nordic cross. Learn the color combinations and they stop blurring together.

  • Denmark: red with a white cross.
  • Norway: red with a blue cross outlined in white.
  • Iceland: blue with a red cross outlined in white. It's Norway with the colors swapped.
  • Sweden: blue with a yellow cross.
  • Finland: white with a blue cross.

Colombia and Ecuador

Both have a wide yellow band on top, then blue, then red, with the yellow taking up half the flag. Ecuador adds its coat of arms in the center, topped by a condor. Colombia's flag is plain. Venezuela uses the same colors, but its three stripes are equal in size and it has an arc of white stars.

Mexico and Italy

Both are vertical green, white and red. Mexico's has its coat of arms in the middle, an eagle perched on a cactus eating a snake, and it's longer, with 4:7 proportions. Italy's is plain and uses 2:3.

Qatar and Bahrain

Both have a white band next to the pole with a zigzag edge. Qatar's main color is maroon and its zigzag has nine points. Bahrain's is red, with five points.

Why so many flags look alike

A few reasons come up again and again. Neighbors and countries with shared histories often borrowed from the same sources. The green, yellow and red of many African flags trace back to Ethiopia's flag. Red, white, green and black are known as the Pan-Arab colors. White, blue and red appear across many Slavic countries, and the Nordic cross ties Scandinavia together.

Simplicity plays a part too. Flags need to be easy to make and recognize from far away, so many use two or three colors in stripes. And sometimes it's coincidence. Indonesia and Monaco arrived at nearly the same red-over-white design separately, and when Indonesia adopted its flag in 1945, Monaco objected. Indonesia kept it anyway.

Practice tips

  • Learn one detail per pair. Don't memorize whole flags. Memorize the difference.
  • Check the stripe by the pole. For tricolors, that stripe is often the giveaway.
  • Count things. Stars, points and stripes settle a lot of arguments.
  • Notice the shape. Some flags are squarer or longer than their lookalikes, which helps when they're shown at their official proportions.
