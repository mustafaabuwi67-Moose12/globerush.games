Want to fly from Atlanta to Tokyo, or from a small mountain town to Paris? Sometimes there's a nonstop. Often there isn't, and you change planes somewhere in between. Knowing why tells you a lot about how airlines work, and it helps you plan better trips.

Hub and spoke

Most big airlines run their networks like a bicycle wheel. They pick a few hub airports and fly from each hub to dozens or hundreds of cities, the spokes. To get from one spoke city to another, you fly through the hub.

The reason is math. Connecting every pair of 100 cities with nonstop flights would take 4,950 routes. Connecting each of them to a single hub takes just 100, and every city can still reach every other one with one stop. Airlines can also fill bigger planes, because one flight into the hub carries people headed to many different final destinations.

That's why the busiest US airports are giant airline bases. Atlanta, Delta's biggest hub, has been the world's busiest airport by passenger numbers in most years of the past two decades. United runs a huge hub at Denver. Miami, a major American Airlines hub, handles more flights to Latin America and the Caribbean than any other US airport.

Point to point

Some airlines, especially low-cost carriers, fly more point to point: city to city, without funneling everyone through one hub. Southwest built its network this way. It's simpler for passengers, but it only works on routes with enough demand to fill a plane on their own.

Why there's no nonstop

If you can't find a nonstop flight, it's usually for one of these reasons:

  • Not enough demand. A route only pays off if planes are reasonably full, day after day. Two mid-size cities far apart may not have enough travelers between them.
  • Distance. Ultra-long flights need special aircraft and are expensive to run. Singapore's nonstops to New York, at around 18 hours, are among the longest scheduled flights in the world.
  • Small airports. A short runway or a small terminal limits which planes can use an airport and where they can go.
  • Seasons. Some routes only run in summer or ski season and disappear the rest of the year.

Nonstops also tend to cost more, partly because airlines know people value the time saved. That's why a one-stop itinerary is often the cheaper choice if you can spare a few hours.

Gateways and geography

Long international flights mostly leave from big hubs and coastal gateways. From a smaller inland city, you'll usually connect first. Some places are natural meeting points because of where they sit on the map. Iceland lies almost exactly between North America and Europe, so Keflavík is a busy stop on transatlantic trips. Dubai, roughly between Europe, Africa and Asia, has been the world's busiest airport for international passengers for years running, and nearby Doha is another major connecting hub.

Geography also explains routes that look odd on a flat map. The shortest path between two points on a globe, called a great circle, bends toward the poles. That's why flights from the US to East Asia often head north over Canada and Alaska instead of straight across the Pacific.

Hubs run in waves

Hub airports aren't equally busy all day. Airlines schedule banks: a wave of flights lands within a short window, passengers change planes, and a wave of departures follows. Icelandair's Keflavík schedule is a well-known example. Overnight flights from North America land in the morning, and a wave of flights to Europe leaves soon after. Later in the day, the pattern runs in reverse.

How to read a departures board

A departures board is full of clues about where you are. That's the whole idea behind Mystery Departures. Here's what to look for.

  • The short hops. Long-haul flights could leave from many places, but small regional flights only go to nearby cities. A board with Chattanooga, Savannah and Augusta is almost certainly in Atlanta. Bozeman, Jackson Hole and Aspen point to Denver. Kona and Lihue mean you're in Hawaii, most likely Honolulu.
  • The airline code. Every flight number starts with a two-letter airline code: DL for Delta, UA for United, AA for American, EK for Emirates, SQ for Singapore Airlines. A board dominated by one airline is probably that airline's hub.
  • The mix of destinations. Lots of flights to Brazil and to Portuguese-speaking Africa suggest Lisbon. A long list of Latin American and Caribbean cities suggests Miami.
  • The language. A board titled BROTTFARIR, Icelandic for departures, gives Iceland away. PARTIDAS points to a Spanish- or Portuguese-speaking country.
  • The times. A cluster of departures within an hour or two is a hub bank in action. Flights from North America to Europe usually leave in the evening and land the next morning.

Plan with hubs in mind

When you compare itineraries, look at which hub each one uses. A connection through a big hub usually means more backup flights if something goes wrong. Be careful with tight connections, and give yourself extra time if you have to clear customs or change terminals during the layover.
